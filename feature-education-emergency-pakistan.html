26 Million Empty Desks: Pakistan's Education Emergency

SP
By Sindh Plus Media Network
October 17, 2026 · 2 min read

Pakistan has the world's second-largest population of out-of-school children: more than 26 million boys and girls who should be in classrooms but are not. It is a number so large it numbs — until you visit a village in Sindh where the government school has no teacher, no roof, and no students.

The reasons are painfully familiar. Poverty forces children into work — brick kilns, workshops, fields. Girls drop out at higher rates, pulled home by household duties or early marriage. In remote areas, the nearest school is hours away on foot, and parents will not send daughters down unsafe roads.

Then there are the ghost schools: buildings that exist on paper, with teachers drawing salaries, but no children inside. Investigations over the years have found thousands of them across Sindh and Balochistan — a theft not just of money but of futures.

"A theft not just of money but of futures — ghost schools steal from 26 million children."

The cost compounds over generations. A child who never learns to read becomes an adult locked out of better work, more likely to marry early, less able to help their own children study. Economists estimate that each year of schooling raises a person's earnings significantly — Pakistan is forfeiting that return on 26 million lives.

There are bright spots. Enrollment drives, stipends for girls, and low-cost private schools have brought millions into classrooms. Community schools in Tharparkar and low-fee schools in Karachi's informal settlements show what works: a teacher who shows up, a safe building, and a parent convinced it matters.

Declaring an education emergency — as governments have repeatedly promised — means little without budgets to match. Pakistan spends a small fraction of its GDP on education, far below what its own policies recommend. Until classrooms compete with canals and motorways for funding, the empty desks will keep multiplying.
