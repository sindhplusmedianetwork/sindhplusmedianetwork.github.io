The Parts of the World That Belong to No Country

Are there still places on Earth that belong to no one? To find out, the BBC spoke to a geology expert and Mark Cooper-Jones of the British YouTube duo Map Men, who explain that the answer depends on how the question is framed. Most of the Earth's surface is sea rather than dry land, so the experts start with the ocean: as soon as you travel 200 nautical miles, about 370 kilometres, from any country's coast, you reach an area known as the high seas.

Under international maritime law, the high seas are essentially for the common good of all humanity, where anyone can fish or carry out scientific research. The idea that no one can own the sea dates back to the seventeenth century, when the Dutch scholar Hugo Grotius wrote an essay called Mare Liberum, meaning free sea in Latin, arguing that you can only own something if it can be used up, and the sea is too vast to become anyone's private property. According to the World Economic Forum, about 43 percent of the Earth's surface falls into this category.

On dry land, almost everything is now claimed by someone, and the experts say the largest exception is in Antarctica: Marie Byrd Land, an area of more than 1.6 million square kilometres that belongs to no country and is claimed by no one. Cooper-Jones explains that it was named almost a hundred years ago by Richard Byrd, a captain in the US Navy and an Antarctic explorer, after his wife Marie, who wrote him letters while he travelled across the vast ice-covered land.
