Can AI issue a fatwa? Islamic app 'Qaf' sparks debate over religion and artificial intelligence

SP
By Sindh Plus Media Network
November 08, 2026 · 2 min read
WhatsApp Facebook X

A heated debate has been going on for several months on social media over the role of artificial intelligence in answering religious questions, after an app called 'Qaf' was launched in the first week of August. According to its App Store description, Qaf is a digital platform designed to answer Muslims' questions and help those who want to ask, learn and reflect. One user on X reacted by writing 'Fear Allah', saying that a person who follows a scholar's wrong fatwa bears no sin, but one who follows an AI fatwa would be responsible for the mistake and answerable before God.

Another user took the opposite view, writing 'If you are a Muslim, download this app', while a promotional video by co-founder Abdul Latif Abdul Fattah was watched millions of times and drew mixed reactions. Days after the launch, Abdul Fattah clarified that there was a big misunderstanding about using Qaf to give fatwas, stating plainly that AI cannot give a fatwa, and that Qaf is only a means of searching for and accessing information. The developers say they did not build or train their own language model, and that Qaf uses existing AI models together with a database of Islamic sources to limit the influence of non-Islamic material.

Al-Azhar scholar Dr Salem Abdul Jalil, a former deputy secretary of Egypt's Ministry of Endowments, told BBC News Arabic that AI research applications have made life much easier for scholars and are useful for students with a firm religious background, but that non-experts should use them for general information and cultural awareness, not for a sharia ruling, a fatwa or settling a dispute. At a conference in Cairo last year on preparing 'the insightful mufti in the age of AI', speakers stressed two urgent needs: benefiting from AI and setting rules for its use, with Dr Muhammad al-Duwaini, speaking for Al-Azhar chief Dr Ahmed al-Tayyeb, calling the use of AI 'a religious necessity' while insisting that machines cannot issue fatwas on their own because a fatwa is an act of scholarly reasoning. Experts broadly agree that the Islamic world should take an active part in developing AI rather than only using it, and that rules are needed to govern the technology, even as researchers warn that Arabic AI models remain weak and that less than half a percent of the internet content used to train large models is in Arabic.
