Pakistan's Last Antique Restorers Give Old Treasures New Life

SP
By Sindh Plus Media Network
October 27, 2026 · 1 min read
WhatsApp Facebook X

Sitting outside his small shop in a warren of alleyways in Rawalpindi, 72-year-old restorer Ahsan Shakeel buffed an antique brass pot to a high shine, pressing it against a mechanised wheel. Shakeel, among the last still practising his craft, quit a job as a hotel cook and followed in his father's footsteps to become an antiques restorer, opening his workshop in the Bhabra Bazaar market in densely populated Rawalpindi in 1980. Today, he is one of the last master restorers preserving antique metalware in Pakistan.

Moving inside his shop, Shakeel navigated wall-to-wall shelves stacked with centuries-old ewer jugs, samovars for boiling, dishes, jars and teapots, most of which were made of copper or brass. He procures the items he restores mostly from scrap yards, ports, or household clearances, and he and his colleague Shakeel Ahmed restore the battered items by hammering out dents, re-tinning the interiors over coal fires, and polishing the metal. A badly damaged item could take a few days to fix, and after restoration a piece can sell between 100 and 1,000 dollars, with customers mostly collectors or antique enthusiasts, including foreigners.

Today, Shakeel mostly relies on online platforms and messaging applications to buy and sell antiques, saying he does not have the same health anymore and travels less. Prices for antiques are on the increase but the number of customers has fallen over time, and Shakeel said the trade is very slow because the money does not turn over quickly. His colleague, 56-year-old Shakeel Ahmed, said he enjoyed restoring worn and damaged objects because they carry stories within them from different communities and historical periods, adding that what they do is like putting life back into the dead.
