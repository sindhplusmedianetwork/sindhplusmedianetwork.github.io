How Taliban Rule in Afghanistan Has Shown Jihadist Movements a New Path

SP
By Sindh Plus Media Network
November 28, 2026 · 2 min read
WhatsApp Facebook X

Nearly 25 years after Al-Qaeda's attacks in the United States on 11 September 2001, and years after the fall of the Islamic State group's so-called caliphate, the landscape of the global jihadist movement has changed considerably, according to an analysis by Mina Al-Lami of BBC Monitoring. The Taliban's return to power in Afghanistan in 2021 was seen by many jihadist circles as evidence that a long armed struggle can eventually lead to power, and since then some jihadist groups have begun focusing more on gaining power at the local level and strengthening their roots in their own areas rather than on a global agenda. Instead of slogans about attacks on Western countries or establishing a global Islamic state, some groups, particularly organisations linked to Al-Qaeda, now appear to be adopting a strategy of expanding their influence in their own regions and finding local allies.

One example cited in the analysis is Al-Shabaab, Al-Qaeda's Somali branch, which claimed in August that one of its so-called charitable organisations distributed rice, pasta, sugar, cooking oil and beans to hundreds of needy families, and the group promoted the activity widely through its social media platforms and radio station. Analysts say this is not only aid work but part of a strategy to win the trust of local people and present the group as a welfare and social force rather than merely an armed jihadist organisation. The analysis notes that this has opened a new debate within jihadist circles, between hardline elements who see any compromise as ideological deviation and a pragmatist faction that regards cooperation with local groups, tribal forces and even former enemies as an effective way to gain power.

Yet the basic question remains, the analysis says, whether this is truly an ideological change or only a new direction in strategy, and whether these groups are changing their goals or trying to achieve the same old objective, a state based on their strict interpretation of Sharia, through new methods. Neither Al-Qaeda nor Islamic State has achieved its larger goal of capturing one or more countries and imposing a system based on its strict interpretation of Islam, and both organisations have suffered major military defeats and serious damage to their leadership over the years.
