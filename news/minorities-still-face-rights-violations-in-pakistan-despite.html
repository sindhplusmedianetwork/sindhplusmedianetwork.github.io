Minorities Still Face Rights Violations in Pakistan Despite New Laws: HRCP

SP
By Sindh Plus Media Network
October 16, 2026 · 2 min read
WhatsApp Facebook X

The Human Rights Commission of Pakistan concluded on Thursday that religious minorities continue to face violations of their fundamental rights — including misuse of blasphemy laws, forced marriages and conversions, hate speech and discriminatory practices — despite recent legislative measures meant to protect them. In its seventh annual report on freedom of religion, titled "Sanctuary Denied: Freedom of Religion or Belief in 2025/26," the HRCP found that scores of individuals accused of blasphemy remain in legal limbo, while alleged perpetrators of an "organised entrapment network" have yet to be investigated. The report documented attacks on the Ahmadi community, including two attacks in Chenab Nagar in October 2025 and June 2026, and pointed to "increasingly overt attempts to exclude Ahmadis" from public and commercial life.

Girls from Christian and Hindu communities continued to face abductions and forced marriages, compounded by poverty and inadequate police responses, while discriminatory employment practices, controversial school curriculum changes and the marginalisation of minority concerns in mainstream media further undermined equal citizenship. At the report's launch, HRCP Director Farah Zia warned that the state's approach to religious minorities was becoming increasingly exclusionary. Minority rights defender Nabila Feroz Bhatti highlighted the case of Kamran Gill, a Christian medical practitioner from Sheikhupura whose dispensary was set ablaze by a mob on October 9 following allegations that he had shared blasphemous content on social media.

HRCP Vice-Chair in Punjab Raja Ashraf said blasphemy accusations had become a "means of exploiting and endangering vulnerable people," while Shehri Pakistan's Arafat Mazhar urged the public to let courts determine legal procedures rather than turning accused individuals into symbols of the blasphemy-law debate, and former HRCP chairperson Hina Jilani said, "Democracy should have no concept of 'minority' and 'majority'." On a positive note, the HRCP welcomed the passage of the National Commission for Minorities Act 2025 and Punjab and Balochistan laws raising the minimum marriageable age to 18 — but noted the minorities commission still hasn't been constituted months after the law's passage. Its recommendations include constituting the commission without delay, publishing annual disaggregated data on blasphemy cases and mob violence, urging Khyber Pakhtunkhwa to set the marriageable age at 18, legal action against incitement to violence, mandatory police and FIA training on freedom of religion, ending recruitment practices that confine minorities to sanitation work, overhauling the Evacuee Trust Property Board, monitoring seminaries linked to forced conversions, holding conversions in court or before a government official, curbing hate speech within the bounds of Article 19, improving policing to prevent mob violence, reviewing school curricula for inclusivity, fully implementing the Supreme Court's 2014 judgment on religious freedoms, and having Section 295-C investigations conducted by senior-ranking officials.
