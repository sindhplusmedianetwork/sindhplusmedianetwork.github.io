Haredi draft refusal deepens Israel's divide and pressures Netanyahu's coalition

Police came to arrest an ultra-Orthodox young man outside a building in Modi'in, between Jerusalem and Tel Aviv, after he refused to present himself for compulsory military service, in an early-October incident recorded on video. Such incidents are among several in recent months that have highlighted a deep division in Israeli society, at a time when Israel is fighting on several fronts, including the unresolved conflict with Hamas in Gaza. The ultra-Orthodox, or Haredi, community makes up about 14 percent of Israel's population, has a much higher birth rate than other Israelis, and could account for one-third of Israel's Jewish population by 2050 if its current growth continues.

The government spends an estimated 35 billion shekels, about 8.4 billion pounds, a year supporting the community so its members can focus on religious education, and until recently its members were exempt from compulsory military service. Two years ago, the Supreme Court ruled that the exemption for Haredi men had effectively ended, prompting mass protests, road closures and clashes with security personnel in densely populated Haredi areas. The traditionally representative parties, United Torah Judaism and Shas, have both been part of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's governing coalition and have sought to block implementation of the ruling, with United Torah Judaism leaving the coalition while continuing to support the government from outside and Shas remaining in it.

Polls put the governing coalition at about 50 to 52 seats, compared with 64 in 2022, while all of Netanyahu's rivals support ending the exemption, and a new Haredi Public Party has proposed a compromise under which full-time Torah students would keep studying and others would serve alongside other young Israelis while retaining their Haredi identity.
