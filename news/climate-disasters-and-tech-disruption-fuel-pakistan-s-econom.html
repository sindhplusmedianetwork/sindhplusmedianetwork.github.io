Climate Disasters and Tech Disruption Fuel Pakistan's Economic Woes, Moot Told

SP
By Sindh Plus Media Network
November 08, 2026 · 1 min read
WhatsApp Facebook X

Federal Minister for Climate Change Dr Musadik Malik said on Friday that climate disasters, technological disruption and an increasingly unequal global economic order are converging to deepen Pakistan's economic vulnerabilities. He was speaking at the concluding day of a two-day conference, Beyond Polycrisis: Pathways Towards a Sustainable Economic Future, organised by IBA Karachi's Center for Business and Economic Research. The minister said Pakistan's economic difficulties could not be understood only through domestic policy failures, as global commodity cycles, interest rates, geopolitical conflicts and technological changes also shaped the country's prospects.

He questioned measuring climate losses only through GDP and financial figures, arguing they should also be assessed through deaths, displacement, disability, disrupted education and destroyed livelihoods. He cited figures of around 6,000 deaths, 20,000 injured or disabled people and 30 to 40 million displaced persons over five major flood events. Referring to flood-hit communities in Gilgit-Baltistan, he said some families had taken three generations to escape poverty, only to lose their homes, livestock, schools and clinics in a single devastating flood.

He also warned that advances in robotics and automation were accelerating industrial change, with serious consequences for workers with low or mixed skillsets. Senator Sherry Rehman, the keynote speaker, said the world was entering a new Axial Age in which climate stress, geopolitical disruption and artificial intelligence were increasingly interconnected, and warned Pakistan was caught in a recovery trap where the next disaster arrives before the previous one is fully addressed. IBA Karachi Executive Director Dr S Akbar Zaidi said Pakistan's population of about 250 million was projected to reach 270 million by 2030, while economic growth had for years remained only slightly above the rate of population growth.
