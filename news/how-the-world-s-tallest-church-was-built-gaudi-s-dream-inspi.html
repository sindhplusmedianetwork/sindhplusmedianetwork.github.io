How the World's Tallest Church Was Built: Gaudi's Dream Inspired by Iraq's Taq Kasra

In June 1926, an elderly man wearing worn-out clothes was crossing a road to go to church when a tram hit him, and he died a few days later in a hospital reserved for the poor. That man was Antoni Gaudi, who later became known as 'God's architect'. Gaudi left behind a great construction project unfinished, as the famous and towering Sagrada Familia church in Barcelona in north-eastern Spain was still under construction at the time of his death.

From a distance the church looks like a giant living being on Barcelona's horizon, and its exterior, decorated with fine carvings, shows events from the Bible as if the designs had emerged from within the rock itself. The construction plan was designed from the start so that work would continue long after Gaudi's death, but those who followed in his footsteps faced extremely difficult challenges. In 1936, during the Spanish Civil War, Gaudi's construction plans and models were destroyed, leaving his successors with very little material and documentation.

At one point it was thought that the church's tall towers were too heavy for their foundations and could be damaged by strong winds. However, nearly a century after his death, the church's central tower, the Tower of Jesus Christ, has been completed at a height of 172.5 metres, and with it the Sagrada Familia has earned the honour of being the world's tallest church. Gaudi was inspired by Taq Kasra, an ancient monument in the city of Ctesiphon in present-day Iraq, an early and prominent example of the catenary arch, a very strong and stable construction structure.

He designed the church's 18 towers on the principle of the catenary arch so that they could bear their own weight. The tower of the Virgin Mary is 138 metres high and is the second tallest tower after the central tower. When its construction was under way, it was found that the pillars below could not bear its weight if traditional stone and reinforced concrete were used, so in 2014 the church's administrators asked the British engineering company Arup for help.

Arup's engineers proposed using relatively thin layers of stone, strengthened by steel wires installed inside them under tension, a method very different from Gaudi's original plan. The construction of the church is still far from complete, and the parts that remain include the main exterior, known as the Glory Facade. Even after completion, modern technology will continue to play an important role in its maintenance: in the past, climbers were used to inspect cracks and damage, and a full inspection took two years, but now drones and artificial intelligence are used to identify cracks that need repair, and once the AI training is complete the whole church will be scanned in just one month.
