Is Poisonous Water the Destiny of Ghotki's People?

AB
By Allah Warayo Bozdar
October 16, 2026 · 12 min read
WhatsApp Facebook X

Once renowned for its fertile land, vast and dense forests, beautiful desert dunes, and sweet groundwater, Ghotki district has gradually deteriorated to such an extent that it is no longer the land famous for producing high-quality cotton. Its once-thriving forests have largely disappeared, and the beauty of its sandy desert belt has also been severely damaged.

Over the past decade, the rapid expansion of the sugar industry has transformed the district from a cotton-producing region into a vast landscape of sugarcane cultivation and a reservoir of polluted water. Despite restrictions, rice cultivation across large areas has further contributed to waterlogging, turning the district's agricultural land into waterlogged terrain.

The forests, meanwhile, fell into the hands of the land-grabbing mafia many years ago. According to Forest Department figures, Ghotki has 37,252 acres of forest land, of which approximately 18,000 acres are occupied by various influential individuals. The Forest Department claims that encroachments have been removed from 17,353 acres, while another 1,919 acres remain to be recovered. The reality, however, appears to contradict these claims. More than 18,000 acres of forest land remain under occupation, and even where forest land is still under the department's control, trees are nowhere to be seen.

Similarly, since 1989, Ghotki's desert belt has been exposed, without interruption, to chemically contaminated wastewater discharged by industrial units operating in Punjab's Bahawalpur and Rahim Yar Khan districts, as well as to saline drainage water from the Sadiqia Canal. As a result, the desert region has increasingly become a dumping ground for toxic water.

"This poisonous water is transported to the Sindh border through three cement-lined canals."

This poisonous water is transported to the Sindh border through three cement-lined canals. From a major pumping station established near Deh Poh 1. in Daharki, large-diameter tube wells discharge the water into Ghotki district's sandy belt. This process continues around the clock. Officials are given weekly briefings at the pumping point about the thousands of cusecs of water that have been discharged.

After approximately 26 years of continuous discharge into Sindh's desert region, this water has inundated a 60–70-kilometre stretch of Ghotki's desert belt and adjoining plains, swallowed settlements and reached the boundaries of Saleh Pat taluka in Sukkur district.

The toxic water coming from Punjab is unfit for human or animal consumption and is unsuitable for agricultural purposes. Its destructive effects include contaminating Sindh's groundwater, making fertile land saline and barren, and submerging settlements, open spaces and agricultural fields.

The people of Ghotki are not merely witnessing these devastating consequences; they are experiencing them firsthand and suffering from their effects. Many have rapidly lost, or are continuing to lose, their agricultural land. People who consume contaminated groundwater are reportedly suffering from kidney, liver, heart and eye diseases, as well as cancer. Poverty and financial hardship leave many unable to afford treatment, and some die before they can receive adequate medical care.

Alongside the damage caused by toxic water coming from Punjab, fertiliser factories, gas companies, power plants and the rapidly expanding sugar mills operating in Ghotki have also contributed to the contamination of the district's groundwater.

Ghotki is home to two major fertiliser factories, four power plants, two gas companies and six sugar mills. These industrial units have been earning billions of rupees for years. Yet, in return for this enormous wealth, the local population has received neither adequate employment nor meaningful development in its towns and villages. The people have not been provided with sufficient healthcare or educational facilities.

"If they have received anything, it is this: the sweet water of their district has turned poisonous."

For years, drinking water in major towns and villages, including Daharki, Jarwar, Dad Lagari, Reti, Khanpur Mahar and their surrounding areas, has been contaminated. Now, nearly half of the district's total population is reportedly being forced to consume polluted water.

The reason is that, in their pursuit of greater profits, the managements of industrial units have avoided investing in wastewater treatment plants required under environmental regulations. Instead, from the very beginning of their operations, they have resorted to makeshift arrangements, discharging chemically contaminated waste into agricultural irrigation canals, ponds and open spaces surrounding their factories.

As this practice continued for years, the pollutants gradually penetrated deep into the soil. Ghotki's groundwater became contaminated.

Major irrigation channels in the district have effectively been turned into sewerage lines for these factories. These include the Ghotki Feeder, the main canal originating from Guddu Barrage, and its subsidiary channels, including Naro Wah, Masu Wah, Qazi Wah, Mehro Wah, Dangro Wah, Bago Wah, Narli Minor and several smaller irrigation channels.

In much the same way, the municipal administrations of Ghotki's towns have adopted the practices of industrial operators and outside investors. They have turned irrigation canals passing through residential areas into open sewerage channels.

In Ghotki and Mirpur Mathelo, for example, the Bago Wah and Old Masu Wah, which pass through the middle of the towns, receive more wastewater from household bathrooms and toilets than water from the river.

Public protests against groundwater contamination have taken place at different times. However, the alleged polluters are outsiders, while influential local landlords and political figures sitting in assemblies are, directly or indirectly, accused of supporting them. In simple terms, they are acting as intermediaries for these interests. As a result, the district administration is also perceived as siding with the industrialists.

Consequently, public complaints have failed to produce meaningful action from the relevant institutions.

But enduring exposure to poison for such a long period, and watching relatives and friends fall ill and die from various diseases, is itself a form of consuming poison in installments.

It is against this background that a group of lawyers from Ghotki has joined hands to seek legal intervention. They have filed a constitutional petition before the Sukkur Bench of the Sindh High Court in the name of Shahnawaz Waseer, President of the District Bar Association.

The petition argues that the discharge of toxic water from Punjab into Sindh, together with the disposal of chemically contaminated industrial wastewater into groundwater and irrigation canals within Ghotki district, has rendered the district's groundwater unfit for drinking. As a result, people and livestock are reportedly dying from illnesses, while the district's agriculture has been devastated.

The petition names the Chief Secretary of Punjab, the Sindh government and the managements of the relevant industrial units as respondents.

During the hearings, legal advisers representing the industrial units appeared before the court and maintained that their factories disposed of wastewater in accordance with applicable rules and regulations. The petitioners rejected these claims.

Subsequently, the court ordered the Pakistan Council of Research in Water Resources (PCRWR) and the Sindh Environmental Protection Agency (SEPA) to collect water samples from various locations throughout Ghotki district, including industrial facilities, in coordination with the petitioners and their lawyers. The samples were to undergo laboratory testing, and a detailed report was to be submitted to the court.

When the commission's team arrived in Ghotki to comply with the court's directions, its preliminary tests began producing worrying results.

"According to (PCRWR)initial findindings water of 73/ area of Ghotki is not able to drinking for human beings."

In several areas of Ghotki city, the total dissolved solids (TDS) level in drinking water was found to exceed 500. In Daharki, Jarwar and Khanpur Mahar, the levels were reportedly in the thousands. The article notes that water with TDS levels of approximately 300–500 is generally regarded as potentially acceptable for drinking, while a range of 50–300 is described as preferable for safer drinking water. However, TDS alone cannot establish whether water is safe: the presence of other contaminants must also be assessed through laboratory testing. The situation became even more troubling when the sampling team visited a fertiliser factory in Daharki. The factory management refused to allow the team to enter either the factory premises or its residential colony.

Not only was the team denied entry, but the lawyers accompanying the petitioners were also allegedly treated disrespectfully.

In response, the lawyers staged protests first at the factory gate and subsequently outside the Daharki police station. They also held a sit-in, but no effective relief was forthcoming. The situation appeared to reflect the familiar saying that "the powerful wield a stick with two ends"—a reference to the unequal treatment of ordinary citizens and influential interests.

The factory management subsequently filed a miscellaneous application in the same High Court petition through a lawyer from Sindh. It argued that families lived inside the factory's residential colony and, therefore, the petitioners' lawyers should not be permitted to enter it.

The court observed that its order already stipulated that the petitioners and their lawyers should accompany the water-sampling team, and that samples should be collected from the locations identified by the petitioners.

That next hearing expected to be significant for the people of Ghotki, whose lives and future are at stake.

How much poison will they be forced to drink, and for how long? And how many more dollars will domestic and foreign industrial investors earn at their expense?
