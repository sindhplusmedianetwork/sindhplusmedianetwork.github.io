Thar's Black Gold: The Coal Beneath Sindh's Desert

SP
By Sindh Plus Media Network
October 17, 2026 · 2 min read

Drive east from Karachi into Tharparkar and the landscape turns to rolling dunes, thorn trees and villages that wait months for rain. Beneath this desert, geologists say, lies one of the largest coal deposits on earth — an estimated 175 billion tonnes, enough to power Pakistan for generations.

For decades the coal stayed in the ground while Pakistan burned expensive imported fuel to keep its lights on. Then, in the last ten years, the first open-pit mines were carved into the desert near Islamkot, and power plants rose beside them. Thar's coal finally began feeding the national grid.

The promise was enormous: cheap domestic energy, thousands of jobs, and development for one of Pakistan's poorest districts. Some of it has materialized. Mining companies built roads, a new airport, schools and hospitals. Young men from Thar who once migrated for work found jobs as drivers, technicians and engineers.

"Thar's coal can light Pakistan's homes — but only if the wealth beneath the dunes also transforms the lives above them."

But the desert's people have paid a price too. Villages were relocated to make way for expanding mines. Groundwater — already scarce — faces new pressure, and farmers worry about the dust and the changing water table. The jobs, locals say, too often go to outsiders while Tharis watch from the fence.

Environmental questions hang over the project. The world is moving away from coal while Pakistan digs deeper into it, and Thar's plants add to the country's carbon burden. Supporters counter that Pakistan contributes almost nothing to global emissions and deserves the same cheap energy that built richer nations.

The honest answer lies somewhere between celebration and warning. Thar's coal can light Pakistan's homes, but only if the wealth beneath the dunes also transforms the lives above them — with clean water, real jobs for locals, and a plan for the day the mines run quiet.
