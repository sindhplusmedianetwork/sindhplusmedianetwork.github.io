The Women Who Walk for Water: Ghotki's Unseen Crisis

By Tahir Adnan
October 10, 2026 · 6 min read

Every morning before sunrise, in villages around Mirpur Mathelo in District Ghotki, women pick up empty clay pots and plastic canisters and begin walking. Some walk two kilometres. Some walk five. They are not going to work or to market. They are going to fetch the one thing no human can live without — water fit to drink.

I grew up watching this. The groundwater beneath our feet is brackish — too salty to drink, and in many places contaminated. A glass of it leaves a white crust on your tongue. Children who drink it fall sick with stomach infections; I have seen it happen in my own extended family, season after season. And yet, year after year, the tenders are announced, the schemes are inaugurated, and the water still does not come.

What strikes me most, as a journalist, is who carries this burden. It is almost never the men. It is mothers, daughters, elderly women — walking in the heat of Sindh's summer, balancing twenty litres on their heads, because the hand pump in the courtyard gives water that no one dares to swallow.

"The water crisis in Ghotki is not a future threat. It is a daily punishment — and women serve the sentence."

Engineers will tell you the solution is simple: reverse-osmosis filtration plants. The technology exists, the need is documented, and the cost of one plant is a fraction of what is spent on less urgent schemes. What is missing is not knowledge. It is will.

I have written to the World Bank, to federal ministries, to anyone whose mandate touches clean water. Some replied politely. Most did not reply at all. But I keep writing, because the women walking at dawn cannot wait for the system to notice them.

A journalist's job, I was taught, is to make the invisible visible. So here it is, plainly: in one of Pakistan's most gas-rich districts, mothers are still walking miles for a drink of clean water. That is not a natural disaster. That is a choice — made by everyone who looked away.

The walk begins again tomorrow at dawn. The question is whether anyone with the power to end it is finally watching.

Tahir Adnan

MA Journalism. Founder of Sindh Plus Media Network. He reports on water, rural Sindh, and the stories mainstream media misses.
